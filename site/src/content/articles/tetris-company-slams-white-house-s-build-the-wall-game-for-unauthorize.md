In a recent move that has sparked major backlash, the **White House launched an arcade section on its website** featuring re-skinned versions of popular games, including a Tetris-inspired clone called **'Build the Wall.'** The game, centered on stacking blocks to fend off a 'zombie border siege' at the 'Southern Border,' was met with swift disapproval from both industry figures and the public for its political messaging.

## Developers and Publishers Push Back

The **Tetris Company quickly distanced itself from 'Build the Wall,'** making it clear via Instagram that it had zero involvement in the game's creation and emphasized its core values of connection and unity. The company also strongly hinted at potential copyright infringement, declaring it takes such matters very seriously. This isn’t the first time publishers have objected: The Pokémon Company similarly denied granting permission for a government-run Pokémon mashup, and **Halo’s co-creator Marcus Lehto** labeled the use of his creations in political propaganda as "absolutely abhorrent."

## Political Messaging Meets Gaming

The use of games for overtly political or governmental messaging isn’t new, but the recent wave—ranging from **Pokémon parodies by the Department of Homeland Security** to memes based on **Call of Duty and Grand Theft Auto to promote US interests abroad**—has called attention to a contradiction: many political voices previously critical of 'politics in games' now find themselves leveraging the medium for ideological communication. The White House’s new arcade section, packed with knock-offs of titles like **Flappy Bird, Snake, and Tetris**, is the latest flashpoint in an ongoing debate over whether video games can—or should—be appropriated for political messaging, especially when bypassing official rights holders.
